Use Case 01 Togal.AI: AI-Powered Takeoff Tool

Tool used: Togal.AI

What it is: Togal.AI is an AI-powered takeoff platform that helps users extract quantities from construction drawings. In this activity, the tool was used to test how well AI could identify building areas, openings, walls, fixtures, foundation elements, and other takeoff items from different types of drawings.

How I used it

I used Togal.AI to review several project drawing sets and compare the AI-generated takeoffs with manual review. The purpose was not only to test whether the software could produce quantities quickly, but also to understand where human review was still needed.

The drawings tested included a military office, fire station, multistory hotel, youth center, and car dealership. These examples included floor plans, reflected ceiling plans, foundation plans, and exterior elevations.

Documents tested

  • Military Office — Floor Plan and Reflected Ceiling Plan
  • Fire Station — First and Second Floor Plans
  • Multistory Hotel — Level 1 Floor Plan
  • Youth Center — Foundation Plan
  • Car Dealership — Exterior Elevations

Key observations

Togal.AI was useful for quickly identifying major building areas and producing an initial takeoff. However, the exercise showed that AI-generated takeoffs still require careful human checking. Some elements were missed, some were misclassified, and some drawings were harder for the tool to interpret than others.

  • On the military office drawing, some windows were not accounted for in the floor plan. In the reflected ceiling plan, fluorescent strip lighting was incorrectly included in the wall perimeter calculation.
  • On the fire station drawings, windows and curtain wall areas were not fully captured, so they had to be manually counted and added.
  • On the multistory hotel drawing, windows were not captured in the AI takeoff and had to be manually counted.
  • On the youth center foundation plan, different foundation types were not identified by the AI, so each footing type had to be counted and adjusted manually.
  • On the car dealership exterior elevations, the AI struggled to read the elevation sheet, so selected walls, windows, doors, and curtain wall elements had to be accounted for manually.

Manual corrections made

The manual corrections showed that the AI output should be treated as a starting point, not a final takeoff. The corrections included adding missed windows, revising curtain wall and facade areas, removing incorrectly included ceiling elements, adjusting area calculations, and manually counting foundation and elevation components.

What I learned

This use case showed that AI can improve speed during quantity takeoff, especially at the early review stage. However, it also showed that construction drawings still require professional interpretation. AI may identify large areas quickly, but it can miss openings, confuse drawing features, struggle with less-developed drawings, and misread elevation or foundation information.

The main lesson is that AI-powered takeoff tools can support estimators and project teams, but they should be used with human verification. The value is strongest when AI handles the first pass and the professional checks, corrects, and interprets the output.

Use Case 02 Reviewing Custom-Built Mini Agents

Tools reviewed: Harvard MBA – Consulting Management and Construction GPT

Purpose of the use case: This activity reviewed two custom-built GPTs to understand how specialized mini-agents perform on different AEC-related tasks. The first GPT was tested on a capital project recovery problem, while the second was tested on a job hazard analysis for a renovation project.

Mini-agent 1: Harvard MBA – Consulting Management

I used the Harvard MBA – Consulting Management GPT to work through a $1.2 billion hospital project with cost and schedule overruns. The tool handled the project controls side well. It calculated CPI, SPI, and EAC correctly, and when I asked for the math needed to reach a final CPI of 0.92, it showed that the remaining work would need to perform around CPI = 1.05.

That was the most useful part because I could check the calculation myself. However, when the prompt stayed broad, the GPT shifted into general executive language and began referencing sources I did not ask for. At that point, it felt more like a business case discussion than a focused construction project controls analysis.

This made sense because the GPT appeared to be built more for general management consulting than for niche construction project controls. I still found it useful for this type of problem, but only when the questions were specific and focused.

Mini-agent 2: Construction GPT

The Construction GPT was more practical and closer to how the field actually thinks. I used it to develop a Job Hazard Analysis for a renovation project. It broke the work into clear task steps and flagged hazards that mattered for a night-shift project, including sprinklers, live utilities above ceilings, dust migration, and fatigue.

It also converted the information into a one-page format that a foreman could realistically use, which was exactly what I wanted. However, it still needed follow-up prompts to tighten responsibility and verification so that everything was not placed on the foreman. It also needed prompting to make the final output more concise.

What I learned

This review showed that custom-built GPTs can be helpful, but their usefulness depends heavily on how well the tool fits the task. The consulting-focused GPT worked well for structured project controls calculations, but became too general when the question was broad. The construction-focused GPT was stronger for field-oriented safety planning, but still needed professional review and refinement.

Overall, mini-agents can speed up drafting, analysis, formatting, and structured thinking. However, they still require clear prompts, user judgment, and review by someone familiar with the project context.

Use Case 03 Using a Book Review GPT: Outliers by Malcolm Gladwell

Tool used: Book Summarizer GPT

Book reviewed: Outliers: The Story of Success by Malcolm Gladwell

Purpose of the use case: This activity used a Book Review GPT to summarize and interpret Malcolm Gladwell’s Outliers. The goal was to see how a specialized GPT could help distill the main argument, identify key ideas, and turn a book into a structured review.

AI-generated book review

The generated review explains that Outliers challenges the idea that success is only the result of individual brilliance. Instead, it presents success as something shaped by opportunity, timing, culture, accumulated advantage, and sustained effort. The review compares success to two trees planted in different soils: one grows tall and the other struggles, not necessarily because one seed is better, but because the surrounding conditions are different.

The main idea is that outliers are not simply anomalies of talent. They are often beneficiaries of patterns, systems, and opportunities that make exceptional achievement possible.

Key ideas from the book

The 10,000-Hour Rule

The review explains that Gladwell popularized the idea that mastery requires roughly 10,000 hours of deliberate practice. However, the deeper point is not the exact number. The key idea is accumulated advantage. People who reach mastery are often those who receive early access to opportunities that allow them to practice intensely over time.

The Matthew Effect

The Matthew Effect describes how early advantages compound. Small differences at the beginning can lead to better coaching, more confidence, more practice, and stronger long-term outcomes. What may look like natural superiority is often the result of a system that amplifies early advantages.

Cultural Legacy

The review also highlights cultural legacy. Gladwell argues that attitudes toward authority, risk, communication, and work are shaped by cultural inheritance. These invisible scripts can affect outcomes in areas such as aviation, mathematics, entrepreneurship, and professional performance.

Meaningful Work and Autonomy

Another major idea is that high achievers often engage in work that is complex, autonomous, and meaningful. Autonomy fuels persistence, complexity builds skill, and meaning sustains long-term effort.

Action plan generated by the GPT

  • Engineer deliberate practice: Structure growth around focused, feedback-driven practice rather than waiting for inspiration.
  • Seek and create opportunities: Build proximity to better mentors, networks, and learning environments.
  • Leverage cultural strengths: Reflect on inherited habits, values, and communication styles, then amplify strengths and correct blind spots.
  • Build meaning into work: Choose projects and environments that support autonomy, purpose, and long-term growth.

What I learned

This use case showed that a Book Review GPT can help organize a complex book into a clear structure. It was useful for identifying themes, explaining key concepts, and turning the book into action-oriented takeaways. However, the output still needs human review because a book review should reflect the reader’s own interpretation, not only the GPT’s summary.

For AEC learning and professional development, a tool like this could help students and professionals process books on leadership, productivity, project management, safety culture, innovation, and organizational behavior. It can support learning, but it should not replace reading or personal reflection.

Use Case 04 Creating a Custom-Built Mini-Agent: Construction RFI Analyst

Project: Construction RFI Analyst

What it is: A custom mini-agent built to help users review, summarize, classify, draft, and improve construction Requests for Information.

What it represents: This project demonstrates how AI can support construction documentation workflows by extracting key information, identifying weak or missing details, and improving the quality of project communication.

Why it matters: RFIs are a routine but time-sensitive part of project delivery. A focused AI tool can help reduce repetitive review work, improve the quality of written RFIs, and make requests easier for design and project teams to answer.

Problem the mini-agent was designed to solve

Construction projects often generate many RFIs in a short period of time. Some RFIs are unclear, incomplete, or missing important references, which can slow communication between project teams and delay decisions. The Construction RFI Analyst was created to help users quickly identify the main issue in an RFI, classify the type of request, flag missing information, and improve the wording so the RFI is clearer and easier to answer.

Intended users

This mini-agent is intended for project engineers, assistant project managers, construction managers, design coordinators, interns, and students learning construction administration and documentation workflows.

How it was built

The mini-agent was intentionally scoped only to RFIs so that its responses would stay focused and consistent. Its instructions defined five main tasks: reviewing existing RFIs, summarizing the issue, classifying the likely RFI type, identifying missing or weak information, and drafting new RFIs from field issues.

It was also instructed not to invent project facts, drawing references, specification sections, cost impacts, or schedule impacts when that information was missing. This was important because RFI work depends on project-specific information, and an AI tool should not fill gaps with unsupported assumptions.

Core responsibilities and output structure

The mini-agent was instructed to extract key RFI details, summarize the issue, classify the RFI type, flag missing or weak information, and suggest clearer wording when helpful. For RFI drafting, it was instructed to produce a clear title, issue description, question, references provided, and missing information needed.

The RFI types used for classification included design coordination, design change, design clarification, scope deletion, construction coordination, and site condition RFIs.

Knowledge and references used

The setup was supported by three main references: an Autodesk Construction Cloud RFI template, sample RFIs, and an RFI writing guide created for this project. These materials helped ground the GPT in standard RFI structure, common RFI categories, writing principles, and common weaknesses such as vague wording, missing references, and unclear locations.

The RFI template showed typical fields such as project number, project name, requesting party, assigned party, RFI title, request date, due date, priority, cost impact, schedule impact, question, references, category, response, and signature fields.

Testing and performance review

The mini-agent was tested in three separate threads. Across the tests, it stayed within the purpose it was built for. It was able to review existing RFIs, identify missing details such as weak references and unclear locations, pull out stated cost and schedule impacts, and improve a vague draft RFI into a clearer and more answerable request.

It was most useful for repetitive RFI-related tasks such as reviewing wording, flagging unclear references, identifying missing information, and improving draft RFIs. It also generally avoided inventing project information and used placeholders when details were missing.

Limits and professional judgment

The project also showed that AI still needs human review. In testing, the mini-agent could become repetitive and sometimes treated placeholder text as if it were real project information. It could improve wording, but it could not recover missing project-specific details such as exact locations, drawing references, or specification sections that were never provided.

This reinforced an important lesson: AI can be useful as a support tool in construction documentation, but it is not a substitute for project knowledge, responsible review, or professional judgment.

Ethical and practical considerations

A mini-agent like this should be used carefully because RFIs can affect coordination, schedule, cost, and accountability. If someone relies on the output too quickly without checking it, weak assumptions or mistakes could carry over into actual project communication. Privacy is also important because project documents may contain information that should not be uploaded into a public AI tool.
